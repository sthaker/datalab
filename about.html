Why this curriculum

For parents. Last updated 11 November 2026.

In two minutes

School math is built to reach calculus. Data and statistics get the time left over, and national scores in that strand fell between 2019 and 2022. Data Lab is a home course that runs next to school to cover it. It replaces nothing.

Two kids, two tracks. A 4th grader does three sessions a week of about 10 minutes, on paper with real objects, then a short check on a screen. A 7th grader does three of about 15 minutes, mostly in CODAP, a free browser tool for exploring data. Each track opens with a prediction. Every session ends with a check that decides whether it counts. Old questions return at widening gaps. Each six-session unit ends with a quiz that includes unseen questions. Both kids collect their own data and finish with a piece of work a parent signs off.

The content follows UCLA's high-school Introduction to Data Science course and the American Statistical Association's GAISE framework. The mechanics rest on two findings with strong evidence, quizzing and spaced review, and on several with weaker support: predicting first, mastery gates, concrete materials.

The trial is four weeks and twelve sessions per kid. It tests whether the kids come back and finish without a parent sitting there. Nine or more sessions done mostly alone, and a kid who wants to continue: we continue. Fewer than six, or a kid who does not want to: we stop. In between, we adjust and run two more weeks.

What is unproven: nearly everything specific to this course. The lessons were written days ago with AI help, and no teacher has reviewed them. We know of no normed test of data science at these ages. The quizzes show recall and some transfer. Whether these two kids will direct themselves is the question the trial exists to answer.

The gap in standard school math

The standard sequence runs from arithmetic through algebra and geometry to calculus.

In the Common Core, grades K–5 have a domain called Measurement & Data. Statistics & Probability becomes its own domain in grade 6 and runs through high school. When the standards were drafted, the American Statistical Association (ASA) said it was "concerned with the limited role" of data analysis, probability and statistics in K–5.

The field's own statement of what school statistics should be is the ASA's GAISE II report (2020, endorsed by the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics). It builds three levels, roughly elementary, middle and high school, on one four-step investigation.

On NAEP, the national assessment, scores in data analysis, statistics and probability fell 10 points for 8th graders and 4 points for 4th graders from 2019 to 2022, per an analysis by Data Science 4 Everyone.

The remedy is disputed. California's 2023 math framework includes a chapter on data science. Drafts proposed a separate high-school data science pathway, dropped after university STEM faculty objected that students in those courses would be unprepared for college STEM work. Supporters say such courses keep students in math. In 2024 a University of California faculty workgroup reviewed three data science courses and found that none can stand in for Algebra II. Data Lab sits next to the algebra sequence and takes no side.

What this is modelled on

UCLA's Introduction to Data Science (IDS)

The starting point. IDS is a year-long high-school course built by a UCLA and Los Angeles Unified partnership. Borrowed: the order of ideas, and students collecting data about their own lives. Not borrowed: the lessons, which are written for high-school classrooms, or the labs, which use R and RStudio. IDS is one of the three courses the UC workgroup reviewed.

The GAISE investigation cycle

Formulate a question, collect or consider data, analyze, interpret (GAISE II, PDF). Each track's second unit is one pass through it. For the 4th grader: ask, collect, draw, tell.

Berkeley's Data 8

Foundations of Data Science: a university course in Python with a free online textbook. Where the older track points. A plan.

AP Statistics

The College Board's course and exam: exploring data, sampling and experimentation, probability and simulation, inference. The older track's outside yardstick, years away.

Notice-and-wonder graph routines

The 4th grader's "mystery graph" appears in three stages, and the kid says what they notice. The format follows Slow Reveal Graphs, the ASA and New York Times feature What's Going On in This Graph? and Youcubed's data talks. The graphs are our own.

The design choices and the evidence

The strength labels are ours. Those for quizzing, spacing and concrete materials follow the U.S. Institute of Education Sciences practice guide (2007).

Predict, then test, before any explanation

Session one is a guess, then a measurement: which hand is faster, which color is most common in a blind handful.

Evidence: thin to moderate
The practice guide rates pre-questions "minimal". A 2021 review finds predicting beats restudying for simple facts when feedback is immediate. It does not cover conceptual change.

A check every session, and spaced review

One to three questions close each session. They return as warm-ups after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. A miss resets to 1 day.

Evidence: strong (quizzing), moderate (spacing)
Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated these two highest of ten study techniques, across ages. Our exact intervals are a convention.

Mastery gates before moving on

After six sessions: a 10-question quiz, 9 to pass, five or six questions unseen, unlimited retakes.

Evidence: moderate
Bloom's 1984 paper is titled "The 2 Sigma Problem". A 1990 meta-analysis of 108 evaluations found an average gain of 0.52 standard deviations, from upper elementary to college. Those were taught programs. Ours is a quiz.

The kids' own data

Counts of things at home, a week of sleep, screen time and activity, reaction times.

Evidence: thin
GAISE II recommends questions "of interest to students". That is guidance, not a trial. That it keeps kids coming back is our inference.

Short sessions and a weekly target

8–12 minutes for the 4th grader, 10–20 for the 7th grader, three a week.

Evidence: none cited
A practical choice: this sits on top of school. Nothing cited here says 10 minutes is the right size.

Paper and real objects first for the 4th grader

Each mission is a printed card: sort, tally, draw, measure. The screen is for the check.

Evidence: mixed
The practice guide rates linking concrete and abstract "moderate". A meta-analysis of 55 studies, kindergarten to college, found small to moderate gains from manipulatives, larger for retention than for transfer.

Self-pacing with test-out

A kid can do more than three a week, or take a unit quiz early and skip the unit. Skipped sessions never count as done.

Evidence: thin, partly negative
The 1990 meta-analysis found self-paced mastery programs often lowered completion in college classes. We have nothing to cite at these ages. The trial tests it.

A limited role for the parent

Set up once, ten minutes a week, sign off projects. No sitting in.

Evidence: none cited
A household constraint and a hypothesis.

Left out on purpose

  • Points, badges, leaderboards, pay per session. Our judgment: they teach working for the payout.
  • An AI tutor. The page the kids use has no AI in it. A tutor that hands over answers trains kids to ask for them.
  • Daily streak pressure. The plan is three a week.

The tools

CODAP

CODAP is a data exploration tool from the Concord Consortium. It is free, open source, runs in a browser, needs no account and is designed for grades 5–14.

It shows a table. Drag a column name onto an axis and a graph appears, one dot per row. Click a dot and the same row lights up in the table and in every other graph. The 7th grader starts with a built-in table of 27 mammals.

It needs a computer with a mouse or trackpad. The 4th grader does not use it in the trial.

The Data Lab page

It shows the steps, runs the check, schedules review, graphs the kid's own counts and shows a parent the numbers. It cannot send reminders, see a notebook or a CODAP window, or grade written answers.

Paper

A notebook each. Printed mission cards for the 4th grader.

Later, if this continues

Plans, not built
Python notebooks through the free Kaggle Learn courses for the older track. Khan Academy unit tests as an outside check.

What each track covers in the trial

4th-grade track

  • Guess first, then count.
  • Read bar graphs, picture graphs with a key, and dot plots.
  • Tally in fives. Draw a bar graph by hand with a title, labels and a scale from 0.
  • Tell which questions need data.
  • Find the middle, the most common and the range.

At the end: a hand-drawn bar graph answering a question the kid picked, with three sentences: I asked, I found, I wonder.

7th-grade track

  • One dot is one case.
  • Categorical and numeric data. What makes a question statistical.
  • Compare groups by where most of the dots sit.
  • Shape, median and mean, range and interquartile range.
  • Outliers, and which summary to trust.

At the end: a one-page data story about the kid's own data: question, collection, graph, finding, limit.

The four-week trial and the decision rule

The first question is whether they come back and finish without a parent sitting there. What they learned comes second. Twelve sessions each, then a decision made with the kids.

Continue

9 or more of 12 sessions done, mostly alone, and the kid wants to continue. The next unit gets written.

Adjust

6 to 8 sessions, or 9 or more only with a parent alongside. Change the time slot or shorten the sessions. Run two more weeks.

Stop

Fewer than 6, or the kid does not want to. No make-up work.

What is unproven and what could go wrong

  • The content is unpiloted. It was written days ago with AI assistance and reviewed by a second AI system and by the parents. The kids are finding the mistakes.
  • Novelty fades, usually in week two or three. The page sends no reminders.
  • No normed measure. We know of no test that says where a kid stands in data science at these ages.
  • The quizzes are small. Ten questions show recall and some transfer, not mastery.
  • It depends on a weekly parent check. If that lapses, nobody reads the written answers.
  • "Self-directed" is the premise under test. None of the sources above is a study of kids these ages working alone at home.
  • One borrowed idea has no independent support. Short mastery-based sessions follow Alpha School's description of its model. Its published results are its own claims.

What a parent does

  • Agree a regular slot with each kid.
  • Once a week, open Parent view: check sessions done, read one written answer, sign off any waiting project.
  • To see what the kids see, use "Grown-ups: try the lessons" on the home screen. Nothing is saved.
  • Stay out of sessions. Leave their conclusions alone and ask how they know.
