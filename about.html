Why this curriculum

For parents. Last updated 11 November 2026.

The gap

School math is a ladder to calculus: arithmetic, then algebra, geometry and more algebra. Data gets what is left over. In the Common Core, statistics does not become its own strand until 6th grade. Before that it shares a "measurement and data" strand. The American Statistical Association said at the time that data and statistics had too small a role in the early grades.

It shows in results. On the national assessment, scores in data analysis and statistics fell between 2019 and 2022 for both 4th and 8th graders, more for 8th graders.

This is the math adults use most: reading a chart, judging whether a claim holds up, knowing what an average hides. Data Lab runs next to school to cover it. It is a gap in the standard sequence, not a complaint about any school.

What it is

  • Two tracks, one for a 4th grader and one for a 7th grader. Each kid works alone.
  • Short sessions, three a week: about 10 minutes for the younger track and 15 for the older.
  • Every session starts with a guess or a question and ends with a quick check. The check decides whether the session counts.
  • The kids collect their own data: a handful of things sorted by color, a tally of what a pet does, a week of their own sleep and screen time, their own reaction times.
  • The younger track starts on paper with real things, from a printed card. The screen is for the check at the end.
  • Old questions come back as a short warm-up, at widening gaps of 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days.
  • Each unit ends with a quiz that includes questions they have not seen. The second unit adds a piece of work a parent signs off.

What it is not

  • Not math support. If a kid needs help with school math, that comes first and this is not it.
  • Not an AI tutor. This page has no AI in it.
  • Not a long commitment. The ask is four weeks.

The four-week trial

The first question is not whether they learned statistics. It is whether they come back, and whether they can finish without a parent sitting there. Twelve sessions each, then a decision made with the kids.

At week 4Decision
9 or more of 12 sessions done, mostly alone, and the kid wants to continueContinue. The next unit gets written.
6 to 8 sessions, or 9 or more only with a parent alongsideChange the time slot or shorten the sessions. Run two more weeks.
Fewer than 6, or the kid does not want toStop. No make-up work.

What could go wrong

  • Novelty usually fades in week two or three.
  • The lessons are new. The kids are the first to try them, and they are finding the mistakes. The changelog below lists each one.
  • It depends on a parent looking at the Parent view for ten minutes a week.

What a parent does

  • Pick a regular slot with each kid. The page cannot send reminders.
  • Once a week, open Parent view: check sessions done, read one written answer, sign off any project that is waiting.
  • To see what the kids see, use "Grown-ups: try the lessons" on the home screen. Nothing there is saved.
  • Avoid sitting in on sessions or correcting their conclusions. Ask how they know instead.
